# Stop slop writing

The goal: text that sounds like a person who knows the topic wrote it in one go, not like a model filling a template. Readers spot AI writing in a sentence or two, and once they do, they stop trusting the rest.

## The core rule

**Every sentence carries a new fact, a reason, or the next step.** A sentence that exists for rhythm, drama, a transition or a summary gets deleted. Real writers don't spend a sentence on nothing. They run one thought on with "and", "so", "because", "which" until it's said, then start the next thought.

**One thought per sentence, said fully.** Not chopped into fragments for punch, and not several thoughts glued together with "and… and… so…" into a 50-word chain. When a new thought starts (a new step, a result, a lesson), start a new sentence.

Length as a sanity check: natural human prose in this style averages about 17–25 words per sentence, with almost no sentences of 6 words or fewer and nothing over about 40. Typical AI drafts average about 11 words with lots of tiny sentences. Count it after writing, but judge each sentence on whether it says something.

## The 12 tells (check every sentence against these)

1. **The split sentence.** One fact cut into two short sentences to sound punchy.
   ✗ "Most companies already have hundreds of videos. Some have thousands."
   ✓ "Most companies already have hundreds or even thousands of videos."
   Test: two sentences in a row with the same subject, where the second only extends the first → merge.

2. **The detached list.** A full stop before the list, so the list becomes a fragment.
   ✗ "It makes 150 pieces of content a day. Reels, carousels, stories and articles."
   ✓ "It makes 150 pieces of content a day, everything from reels and carousels to stories and articles."
   Test: a list never starts its own sentence.

3. **The aftermath sentence.** Describes the obvious result of what was just said.
   ✗ "So the videos just sit there." / "The tool sticks to it." / "All of it."
   Test: delete it. If the reader loses no fact, it stays deleted.

4. **The placeholder phrase.** A vague phrase standing in for information that comes later or never.
   ✗ "agents do the rest" / "a few things broke" / "the review side" / "turn it into everything it could"
   ✓ Say the thing right away.
   Test: can the reader answer "what exactly?" If not, replace it with the actual thing.

5. **The opinion stub.** A short verdict with no reason and no new fact.
   ✗ "And I think that's wrong." / "That was the wrong goal." / "It's not magic."
   ✓ "That's not ideal, because there's only one shared agent for the whole company."
   Test: an opinion without a "because" in the same sentence gets cut.

6. **The straw-man negation ("not X, it's Y").** Denies something nobody claimed.
   ✗ "I didn't start with a system. I started with one video." / "That table is a limit, not a list of ideas." / "It isn't just a file. It's a full project."
   Good writers negate only a belief the reader actually holds, and then prove it.
   Test: negate only a belief named earlier, and only with proof after it. Otherwise just say Y. This also covers staccato repetition ("Not this, or this, but that").

7. **The signpost stub.** A standalone fragment announcing the next topic.
   ✗ "So, the results." / "Next, the plan." / "Here's the thing." / "And this is the important part."
   ✓ Attach the transition to content: "Now, what about pricing? The basic plan costs…" (a question that leads straight into the answer is fine).
   Test: a transition never stands alone as a sentence.

8. **The decorative "honestly".** "Honestly", "frankly", "truth be told" as seasoning with nothing admitted.
   Test: only before something that cuts against the writer or against what the reader expects.

9. **The parallel triplet.** Three short sentences or phrases with the same structure.
   ✗ "The text was too clean. The questions felt bolted on. The cuts came too early."
   ✓ One sentence with "and", or pick one item and explain it properly.
   Test: three same-shaped sentences in a row → merge.

10. **The neat bow.** A quotable closing line that sums up and could fit any text.
    ✗ "Ultimately, it comes down to execution." / "And that changes everything." / "The future is here."
    ✓ End on the last fact or the next step.
    Test: if the last sentence could be pasted under a different article, delete it.

11. **A claim without the mechanism.** States a result and moves on.
    ✗ "That's slow, and every step can break." (why?)
    ✓ Walk the logic: "Every command is another chance for the app to hang, so a long job turns into a lot of steps that can each break."
    Test: every claim gets its "because / so", or it gets cut.

12. **Tidied-up roughness.** When editing someone else's text, removing their asides, hedges and side comments.
    ✓ Keep "I don't know what they're doing over there, but for me it's pretty much unusable."
    Never invent asides either. Test: every aside the author had is still there.

## Also never

- **Slogans after a story or example:** "One agent, everybody's information, no walls." The example already made the point.
- **Reactions to your own content:** "Seriously." / "Yeah, a kitchen." / "Funny, right?" / "Let that sink in." Tell what happened and let it speak.
- **AI vocabulary:** delve, unpack, this signals, this underscores, navigate the complexities, ever-changing landscape, synergies, leverage (as a verb), holistic, robust, seamless(ly), empower, unlock, supercharge, game-changer, tapestry, testament to, realm, crucial, pivotal, foster.
- **Paragraph openers:** Moreover, Furthermore, Additionally, Importantly, That said, In conclusion, It's worth noting.
- **Bold-word-colon bullet lists** ("**Clarity:** Ensure your memo is clear.") in prose that should read as prose.
- **The corporate-therapist voice:** "a powerful opportunity to lean into our strengths and foster a culture of…"
- **Excessive adverbs:** "quietly underscores", "deeply transformative". Just say it.
- **Says everything, means nothing:** if you can't summarise a paragraph in one plain sentence, delete or rewrite it.
- **Em-dash drama** as a rhythm device. Use a comma or a full stop.

Short sentences are allowed only for a real reaction to something happening in front of the reader, or a two- or three-word repeat right after a surprising concrete fact that repeats the fact itself ("They made no sales. Zero."). Nothing else.

## Write for the reader's level

- Decide who reads it and what they already know before writing. A "tech-savvy reader" knows what an AI model or GPU is, but not internal library names, chip codes or acronyms from a README.
- Every term the reader wouldn't know gets explained in plain words in the same sentence, or gets left out. Prefer what it does over what it's called: "it splits one big model across many chips" instead of "MoE expert parallelism".
- Don't stack names. One or two concrete names help; a run of four library names is a list for insiders.
- Pick the facts that matter to this reader and say why they matter (what they can do with it, what changes for them). A complete list of every feature reads like a copied README.

## Facts

- Use concrete details: names, numbers, dates, what actually happened. Abstract claims get an example right away.
- Never invent facts, numbers, quotes or results. If you don't know something, find out (search, read the source) or leave it out and say what's missing.
- Plain words over impressive ones: "use" not "utilize", "is" not "lives", the normal word instead of an invented metaphor.

## Process

1. Know what you're saying first: write down who the reader is, the facts that matter to them, and the one point of the text.
2. Write it the way you'd explain it to a smart friend who's in a hurry.
3. Go through every sentence against the core rule and the 12 tells. Delete, merge or rewrite.
4. Read it aloud in your head. If a sentence sounds like an ad, a LinkedIn guru or a press release, rewrite it.
5. If you're editing someone's text, keep their sentences and voice; fix only what breaks the rules, and say what you changed.
